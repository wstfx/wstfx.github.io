01 / From a family need to a product question

Start with daily life.

At Navix, I work on product exploration for AI-assisted care. My early work spanned older adults, family members, account permissions, conversations, and emergency-contact journeys. The current exploration focuses on Xiaoqi: a small companion robot and phone experience that bring health information and everyday routines into one product.

My contribution connects product requirements, interaction design, runnable prototypes, and supporting research. An early sketch helped me frame the relationship: the companion learns about a person’s routines and context, while the person and their family need ways to inspect and correct that understanding.

02 / Keep the product logic visible

Make the design inspectable.

I organized the product into capabilities, actions, and data, then built a design workbench around that structure. Each feature has a stable identity, a description of its inputs and behavior, open questions, and links to the relevant prototype, source, and checks.

This gives a design review a concrete path: choose a feature, inspect its flow, try the available demonstration, and check which parts have actually been implemented. Design approval, code coverage, and successful checks remain separate states. Draft recovery and version-conflict handling also protect work when a feature is edited in more than one place.

03 / Translate the system into a daily experience

A routine, together.

I built the phone prototype in Vue and TypeScript, connecting a companion-led home screen with health information, everyday schedules, and a personal profile. The interface explores a quieter form of assistance: a brief check-in, a readable trend, or the next activity in the day.

Keep the day together.

Meals, movement, personal plans, and health-related entries share one timeline. Choosing a category highlights matching cards without hiding the rest or moving the reader away from their place.

Respect an ongoing routine.

The scheduling prototype supports daily, weekly, and monthly repetition, with edits to one occurrence or the whole series. Completion, cancellation, and rescheduling preserve the distinction between a recurring plan and a particular day.

The phone UI uses synthetic health information. The local scheduling service is functional; automatic notifications, health-device integrations, and the earlier home-screen sample calendar are not a single connected production system.

04 / A helpful reply needs a real outcome

Beyond “done.”

An assistant can say that it has changed a plan even when an operation failed. I worked on making the outcome inspectable: the model proposes a tool call, the service validates and executes it, and the final confirmation is assembled from actual receipts and a read-back of the saved record.

A requestWhat the person asked
A checked actionTool, arguments & revision
A saved resultWrite receipt & read-back
A clear responseSuccess, partial result or failure

The same executor serves the web console, command line, and MCP. Request identifiers prevent duplicate writes on supported retries; revision checks reject stale edits. If saving succeeds but the model later stops, the interface can still report the recorded result. Setting a reminder time also remains distinct from sending a notification.

I connected the console to a real language model and a screen-based robot preview. It exposes tool events and preview acknowledgments, so a successful command is not confused with a physical robot action.

What the recorded model checks establish

On October 5, an isolated 21-case regression batch passed 42 of 42 repeated checks. A later four-case targeted batch passed 7 of 8: one rescheduling request produced invalid parameters, which the service rejected. These are bounded prototype checks, including partial text assertions—not a claim of general intent understanding or production reliability.

05 / Personalization needs a way to say no

A profile people control.

I designed and implemented an independent profile module covering identity, goals, preferences, routines, and health context. It starts empty rather than treating sample dashboard values as facts about the user. “Unknown,” “none,” and “prefer not to answer” are separate states.

People can add or edit a record, skip the guided questions, withdraw personalization, or ask the module to forget information. Each saved item carries source and version information. The profile service remains separate from scheduling; automatic extraction from conversations and cross-domain personalization are future work.

A recurring design principle

The user should be able to see what the system knows, correct it, and understand what actually happened.

06 / Research that changes the design

Connect product choices to evidence.

Alongside the prototypes, I compared six facial health-sensing suppliers and researched audio-content services, looking at integration constraints, available outputs, privacy questions, and cost. I also organized a 27-source health-management reference library and later evidence maps to support product discussions.

The research helps separate a promising interaction from a confirmed capability. Current work has produced requirements, design tools, phone interfaces, and local AI-action workflows. Reliable device integration, real notification delivery, and validated health outcomes remain outside what these demonstrations establish.
