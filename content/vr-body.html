01 / The learning problem

A familiar station. An unfamiliar set of rules.

Special relativity asks learners to reconsider ideas that usually feel obvious: whether something is moving, how long it is, and whether two events happen at the same time. Our project approached these questions through a beginner-friendly VR lesson set at a railway station, with Albert as a conversational guide.

The experience uses a tutorial-style narrative to confront misconceptions step by step. Learners make predictions, observe what happens, change reference frames, and revisit the explanation. The station and train give them a familiar setting to return to as the physics becomes less intuitive.

02 / Learning through comparison

Predict. Change perspective. Reconsider.

The course builds toward a train-and-platform version of the barn paradox: can a train longer than a station fit between its gates? Each act supplies part of the explanation.

  1. Start with everyday motion

    A ball is dropped from a moving train. From the platform, it travels horizontally as it falls; from the train, it falls straight down. Learners switch between the two frames and answer questions about the train’s velocity.

    The teaching move: establish that different descriptions can refer to the same physical event.

  2. Make a prediction at relativistic speeds

    The lesson introduces length contraction and time dilation using the train, the platform, and stopwatches. Slow-motion playback and a reference outline make the comparisons easier to inspect. Learners predict what changes when they move to the other frame.

    The teaching move: connect the prediction to a chosen reference frame, then let the learner inspect it.

  3. Resolve the apparent contradiction

    In the platform frame, the moving train can fit briefly between the gates. In the train frame, the platform is shorter. The resolution is the timing of the gates: closures that are simultaneous in the platform frame occur at different times in the train frame. Both descriptions agree that the train passes without a collision.

    The teaching move: use light signals and replay to show how the event timing changes across frames.

How the lesson supports a beginner

The design includes explanatory feedback after incorrect answers, a second attempt at quiz questions, replayable demonstrations, and summaries learners can read at their own pace. Frame changes use a fade to black. The sequence asks learners to commit to an interpretation before revealing the next comparison.

03 / Scientific consistency

The animation has to teach the right physics

My main contribution was reviewing the physical correctness of the simulation and animation. I checked the relativistic parameters used in the experimental design and helped adjust Unity simulation and animation parameters for physical accuracy and visual clarity.

That review connected the physics with the instructional sequence: what frame is the learner in, what is being compared, and what conclusion does the animation invite them to draw? I also helped refine the learning flow and interactions, supported by a literature review on embodiment, agency, and interactivity.

The concept behind the scene

Events at different places can be simultaneous in one reference frame and occur at different times in another.

Here, simultaneity means equal time coordinates in a specified frame; it does not require the events to occur at the same place. Physics background: Einstein Online ↗

04 / From seeing to understanding

What did the learner actually understand?

I also helped extend the questions in a special-relativity assessment to better probe students’ conceptual understanding. The aim was to connect what we asked with what the VR lesson allowed learners to do: compare perspectives, inspect an event, and explain an initially surprising outcome.

This mattered for evaluating the teaching design. Recognizing a scene or recalling a phrase is different from reasoning correctly after the frame of reference changes. The assessment work aimed to make the learning opportunities offered by VR more visible in what we measured.

05 / Study findings

From an experience to evidence of learning

28study participants

The team evaluated the simulation with pre- and posttests, followed by participant interviews. The extended abstract reports that the simulation was effective in teaching several special-relativity concepts.

Interviews also informed design principles for future simulations and helped reveal how participants reasoned about the concepts.

These are team-level findings. My contribution centered on physical correctness, instructional refinement, and assessment questions. The reported pre/post findings support learning within the experience; they do not, on their own, establish that VR outperforms another teaching format.

06 / Connecting physics and learning

An immersive scene needs a clear learning purpose

This project connected two forms of precision for me: getting the physical behavior right, and asking a question that reveals whether someone understands it. A compelling animation can create a moment of surprise; the explanation and assessment determine what we can learn from that moment.

I became more interested in the connection between an interaction and the reasoning it enables—especially when a learner can change perspective and test an intuition for themselves.
