01 / From an idea to a system

What has to happen for a living delivery system to work?

T-SAT explored engineered Salmonella as a delivery system for targeted cancer therapy. The project brought together biological design, laboratory work, mathematical modeling, and public engagement.

My part was to connect those pieces: translate the proposed mechanism into a model, coordinate dry-lab work, and build the website that explained the team’s research.

02 / My mathematical-modeling work

Follow the mechanism.
Inspect the assumptions.

I developed the mathematical model and wrote its explanation in the wiki. I broke the problem into three connected parts: reaching the tumor, controlling bacterial growth and signaling, and describing the resulting tumor-cell dynamics.

The original numerical results below show how those parts were explored. Select a model to see its question, formulation, and output.

How does delivery change with distance?

I considered circular-plane and spherical tumor geometries, combining bacterial movement with travel distance and immune clearance. Numerical integration and root finding let me examine the distance-dependent function and its critical point.

F₃(d₀) = 0

What happens when the colony reaches a threshold?

The model couples bacterial growth with AHL signaling. When AHL reaches its threshold, a fraction of the bacteria undergoes lysis, releasing Bax; the surviving population begins another cycle. I implemented the event-driven dynamics using SciPy’s ODE solver.

dNb/dt = rNb(1 − Nb/K)

How does the modeled population respond?

The final component connects the bacterial system to a tumor-cell population model. It combines logistic growth with an additional loss term, using the modeled effect of Bax release from the preceding stage.

dNt/dt = rtNt(1 − Nt/Kt) − ηNt

Original figures from our 2024 wiki. Results depend on the stated geometry, parameters, and biological simplifications; the animation above illustrates the mechanism.

From equations to computation

I used NumPy and SymPy to work with the mathematical expressions, SciPy for numerical integration, root finding and differential equations, and Matplotlib to visualize the results.

From computation to explanation

I documented the assumptions, code, plots, and interpretation on the model page, making the computational work part of the team’s scientific story.

03 / My wiki design & development

A research project people can enter, explore, and understand.

I designed and developed the wiki with Flask templates, HTML, CSS, and JavaScript. The homepage introduces the project visually; the rest of the site connects the biological design, experiments, mathematical models, and human-practice work.

Build a route through the science

Page hierarchy and navigation connect descriptions, design decisions, experiments, and results. Visitors can follow the project’s argument or go straight to the material they need.

Make the technical work readable

I wrote and edited wiki content, particularly the model page, and presented equations, code, figures, and explanations together.

04 / Dry-lab leadership & public engagement

Connect the people as well as the equations.

As dry-lab group leader, I organized the group’s work, tracked progress, and coordinated project activities. My contribution also included brainstorming, background research, experimental-data analysis, data curation, writing, and visualization.

Research questions↔Mathematical models↔Public explanations

Beyond the dry lab, I contributed to science popularization, conversations with frontline doctors, and exchanges with other iGEM teams. That work sat alongside the technical development: asking questions, organizing evidence, and communicating the project to different audiences.
