01 / Start with something I can inspect

Make the process visible.

I began by building Fire Sandbox: an interactive environment connecting a changing thermal field, component temperatures, and accumulated damage. I wanted to inspect the chain from an input—such as fire power—to a component’s response and eventual failure.

The workspace lets me adjust a scenario, pause or step through time, select structural members, and inspect their histories. Exterior and cutaway views make the same house readable at different levels: first as a building, then as the frame and components responding inside it.

My work spans the simulation kernel, interactive 3D workspace, run records, and the formulation of the next research study. The current model uses simplified demonstration parameters; physical calibration and validation remain future work.

02 / Connect the state changes

From a heat source to a failed member.

A TypeScript kernel advances a 3D thermal grid with diffusion, a heat source, and heat loss to the surroundings. Structural members sample that field and heat up with thermal inertia. Their temperature histories drive cumulative damage; crossing a threshold records a failure event once.

The Three.js view connects those events to Rapier rigid-body motion. Released components fall and collide; sufficient impact can break roof panels into predefined fragments. A lightweight smoke model adds a visible plume and upper-layer accumulation. This makes consequences inspectable, while keeping the limits clear: member release is a rule-based demonstration, and the smoke is not a CFD solution.

03 / Cooling does not rewind the experiment

A structure has a history.

Turning off the fire is a useful test of the state model. Temperature can fall while damage remains. In the cooling preset below, the left column continues warming briefly after the source is switched off, and its accumulated damage persists as it cools.

I separated numerical time from playback speed and recorded the initial configuration, seed, and timed interventions with each run. That makes a visual result traceable to an experiment. Checks in the prototype cover numerical stability, irreversible damage, once-only failures, and seeded behavior.

04 / Give the experiment a scene

From a reference to an editable draft.

I also built a reference workspace for images and video frames alongside a parameterized house. The current editor can change dimensions, openings, and appearance within a one-story gable-roof template, then generate the corresponding frame and collision geometry.

A structured VLM proposal interface is wired into the prototype, but reliable reconstruction from real imagery is still an open task. Any proposed geometry or parameter must remain inspectable, editable, and distinguishable from an observation or a default assumption.

05 / The graduation research direction

Begin from the building’s present.

The prototype led me to a broader question. A building may already be burning when observations arrive, and its digital model may be incomplete. Could available images, floor plans, and building documents help initialize a simulation quickly enough to support a useful short-term forecast?

Research question · in development

How can multiple sources of evidence reconstruct the scene and its current fire state, while making conflicts and uncertainty visible?

The planned pipeline combines three kinds of information: geometry and openings; material, source, and boundary parameters; and the current thermal and smoke state. A floor plan can inform geometry, while a recent observation may contradict it. An already-heated building also needs a current-state estimate—starting every run at ambient temperature would discard its history.

I am framing language and vision models as tools for organizing evidence and proposing structured candidates. Geometric checks, state estimation, and a numerical solver would then determine what the simulation actually uses and how it evolves.

06 / An experiment to build next

Test whether better initialization helps.

Controlled evidence.

Start with one or a few rooms and vary what is available: partial views, missing documents, outdated plans, and conflicting sources. Compare vision-only initialization, simple source fusion, verified fusion, and manual setup.

Useful prediction.

Measure scene and opening errors, downstream temperature and smoke estimates, manual effort, and total processing time. A forecast only helps if it arrives with enough useful time remaining.

This evaluation is planned. The working sandbox provides an inspectable starting point; the next stage is to test initialization against a reference simulation and determine which uncertainties matter most. It is not yet a validated fire-safety or structural-collapse prediction system.
