01 / The question behind the work

Start with a life.

My first visit to a special-education college led to a sustained interest in DHH people’s lives. I organized a summer fieldwork project, then worked under faculty supervision on a qualitative study of student happiness. Alongside that work, I explored the AI landscape for the same community.

A question connected these strands: what makes a life feel good, and what makes those experiences accessible? Looking only at hearing loss can obscure the friendships, quiet moments, creative work, and ambitions that people themselves value.

02 / Make room for people’s own accounts

Draw, describe, understand.

The happiness study used graphic elicitation: 39 DHH students at a special-education college in Zhejiang drew their three happiest moments and described them. The drawings and accompanying narratives, collected in 2023–2024, gave us a way to explore experiences beyond a single happiness score.

01 / Elicit

Three happy moments

Start with a drawing and the student’s account of what it means.

02 / Attend

Five questions

When? Where? What happened? With whom? Why was it happy?

03 / Interpret

Patterns across stories

Use thematic analysis to connect everyday situations with psychological mechanisms.

I led the student research work, designed interviews, coordinated fieldwork and research materials, and conducted thematic analysis. Writing the manuscript meant moving between particular experiences and broader themes while preserving the meaning of participants’ accounts.

The 39-student manuscript sample is distinct from our broader 2024 fieldwork and the 52-response communication-preference survey below. Each answers a different question.

03 / The everyday is the finding

Time together.
Room to be yourself.

Happiness appeared in everyday moments, festivals, seasons, and major life events. Family and home-based leisure were especially prominent, alongside friendship, cultural and creative activities, learning, and work. The accounts also showed a balance between social connection and time alone.

Playing. Resting. Learning. Creating.
Spending time with people who matter.

Everyday participation was central to the students’ accounts of happiness.

These sources of happiness closely resemble those of hearing peers. Communication access and inclusion influence how people reach them; hearing loss does not, by itself, define what a happy life contains.

Relatedness

Feeling loved, understood, and part of a family, friendship, or community.

Serenity

Having space for peace, relaxation, and a life with less pressure.

Competence

Feeling capable—in study, work, hobbies, and everyday tasks.

Autonomy

Making your own choices and having a say in how you live.

Engagement

Becoming absorbed in an activity that matters to you.

These are qualitative themes from the manuscript, rather than a ranking or a measured causal model. The illustrations interpret the themes; they do not reproduce participants’ drawings.

04 / From individual moments to a social setting

What surrounds a happy moment?

A family outing depends on more than affection. A first job involves more than individual ability. Our visits to schools, households, disability services, and community organizations helped me understand the support that makes participation possible.

The manuscript’s implications extend across these settings: protect family connection while supporting autonomy, make school social life inclusive, and expand access to leisure, learning, and work. Support is most meaningful when it enables people to participate and make choices.

05 / Put the technology back in context

Where does AI fit?

I also co-authored a systematic review of AI for DHH people. The literature and our fieldwork materials brought several application areas into view: environmental sound alerts, AI-assisted hearing, and communication between signed, spoken, and written language.

Notice

Environmental sounds

Recognize a meaningful sound and communicate it through a visible or tactile alert.

Hear

Hearing assistance

Improve speech enhancement, noise handling, and personalization in assistive listening.

Communicate

Language access

Connect speech, text, and sign language through recognition and generation.

Each direction raises a different design question. Which sounds matter in context? Can a hearing system handle a noisy classroom? Does a translation preserve the grammar, facial expression, and body movement that carry meaning in sign language?

The strong preference for speech-to-text was a useful reminder to look beyond an appealing technical pipeline. Some fieldwork conversations also described the convenience of transcription and the familiarity of sign language: different forms of access can serve different situations.

Language belongs to a community.

Regional variation, scarce training data, and non-manual expression complicate sign-language systems. Evaluation needs to include people who use the language.

Accuracy is one part of usefulness.

Hardware burden, social acceptance, cost, and communication preferences shape whether a promising system fits daily life.

For me, the connection to the happiness study is a design question: can a tool help someone join a conversation, learn, work, or act independently? The review maps possibilities and challenges; building and evaluating an integrated support system remains future work.
