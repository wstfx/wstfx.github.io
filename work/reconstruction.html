Reconstruction beyond one sensor path

How can a moving sensor help us understand an entire flow field—and how can a model keep up when its path changes?

Learning where to look

A moving sensor sees only a small part of a physical field at any moment. In these flow simulations, it collects local velocity and pressure measurements along its path; a reconstructor uses that history to estimate the field across the domain. My early question was whether a more deliberate path could make a limited observation budget go further.

I explored several heuristics. One alternates between following local gradients and moving toward unvisited regions. It briefly observes newly encountered features, then leaves; repulsion, a cooldown, and an oscillation-escape rule help prevent repeated visits to the same neighborhood.

When fewer observations change the answer

The heuristic had an advantage at the smallest observation budgets in this experiment. With more steps, systematic scanning overtook it. This shifted my attention from finding a universally best path to asking which strategy fits the available budget.

This suggests a direction worth testing: when a domain is too large to scan thoroughly, finding informative structures early may matter more. Larger-domain and sparse-target search remain future experiments; the current result concerns reconstruction under limited observation budgets.

Local features, global coverage

Following an interesting feature can spend observations in an already explored region. I compared paths, visited cells, and reconstruction errors to understand why simpler scan patterns remained strong. In these experiments, broader spatial coverage was associated with lower final reconstruction error.

A path the model has never learned

These questions led us to a second obstacle: some reconstructors are trained on a fixed sensing trajectory. Changing the path can change the observation interface enough to hurt reconstruction—even when the new observations contain useful information. Evaluating a sensing strategy therefore also requires understanding the reconstructor’s familiarity with its inputs.

Retraining a reconstructor for new sensing configurations can be costly. We asked whether we could improve trajectory generalization while keeping the original reconstructor frozen. This became the team’s Trajectory Translator (TT) idea and our ICLR 2027 submission.

The team’s approach

Deployment observations → Trajectory Translator → virtual observations on the original interface → frozen reconstructor.

TT learns to produce virtual inputs that help the existing model use the deployment evidence. These inputs need not reproduce the measurements that would actually have been collected on the original path. A teacher optimizes virtual observations with full-field supervision during training; a student learns to predict them from deployment observations.

Giving the idea a strong comparison

My main contribution to the paper’s experimental investigation was exploring competitive baselines. I wanted to test how far alternative solutions could go: could randomized-trajectory fine-tuning make the existing model more flexible? Could a traditional statistical method already reconstruct well from these observations?

I explored randomized-trajectory fine-tuning (RFT), an empirical Gaussian-process (GP) baseline built from training-field statistics, and a fixed-weight fusion of their predictions. RFT updates the reconstructor’s weights, whereas TT keeps it frozen; these represent different adaptation choices.

RFT reduced MSE by 19.6% on Rot90 and 25.8% on Rot180 relative to the frozen model, while error on the original Scan trajectory increased by about 10.4%. Empirical GP was particularly strong under the evaluated trajectory shifts. Fusion improved some comparisons, but did not consistently beat GP on non-scan paths.

On the 112 validation fields excluded from fusion-weight selection, fusion reduced the mean MSE across the three canonical trajectory families by 18.6% relative to RFT. These were development-validation results. The purpose was to establish a demanding reference for TT and understand each approach’s trade-offs.

From the investigation to Trajectory Translator

The team evaluated TT across multiple physical-field benchmarks and reconstructor architectures. The manuscript reports up to 88.9% lower relative reconstruction error for the Transformer on the plate-strain benchmark. In the measured offline settings, translation added 0.39–0.57 ms per complete sequence. These are team-level results under the paper’s evaluated settings.

Back to active sensing

Our next direction is to use TT as a foundation for studying active sensing: choosing where to observe next while continuing to reconstruct the flow field. The goal is to reduce the confounding effect of interface mismatch, then investigate how exploration, coverage, and observation budgets should shape sensing decisions.

For me, this project connected two questions that need to be studied together: where should a sensor look, and how can a model make use of what it finds?
