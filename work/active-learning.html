Choosing what a model learns next

01 / A limited training budget

Which sample is worth learning from?

A model’s training data determines what it has a chance to learn. In scientific machine learning, producing another labeled example can mean running another numerical simulation. I wanted to investigate whether choosing examples in response to the model’s current behavior could make that training budget go further.

My independent research began with a neural query policy for a continuous sample space. It developed into a second approach: self-supervised error-reduction sampling, followed by experiments on how to complete a batch selected by another method.

02 / The first approach

Can a network learn what to ask?

I prototyped a neural-network / reinforcement-learning query strategy that proposed inputs in a continuous domain. The design connected three kinds of information: a query set, the target network, and the accumulated dataset.

The active-learning network proposed a sample, while uniformly sampled candidates provided a comparison. A numerical simulator supplied targets; an efficiency comparison fed the selected example back into target-model training and supplied a training target for the query network.

03 / A useful failure mode

Good proposals were not yet a reliable policy

The query network could propose useful samples, but proposal quality fluctuated. In the archived run below, many proposals rank highly, while repeated drops interrupt that pattern. The result gave me a reason to investigate the policy’s stability rather than judge it only by its best selections.

The broader investigation exposed data hunger, catastrophic forgetting, and brittle gains. Training a sampler also introduces its own learning problem: it has to remain useful as the target model changes.

04 / The revised approach

Make the learning signal more direct

I shifted toward predicting error and using it to guide selection, developing self-supervised error-reduction sampling. The question became more focused: could feedback about error help identify examples that improve the model’s next update?

I compared the resulting sampler with random selection and uncertainty- and diversity-based methods. The figures below preserve two different experimental settings: an MLP on a transcendental-equation toy problem, and a Fourier Neural Operator on Burgers’ equation.

These are archived runs, without multi-seed uncertainty or runtime comparisons shown. They support a promising result in the plotted settings; they do not establish a general speedup.

05 / Refining the question

Improve the batch, one selection at a time

I then explored using error-reduction sampling to complete a training batch generated by an existing active-learning method. This let the initial strategy establish the batch, with error-driven selection providing a complementary step. Coreset emerged as the preferred base strategy in that investigation.

The shift was from asking one learned policy to solve the entire selection problem to studying where an error-reduction step could add value within an existing training process.

06 / Research judgment

A better question for the next experiment

I designed and prototyped the query-policy approach, developed the later error-reduction sampler, ran comparative experiments, and examined proposal rankings, sample distributions, and learning curves. I also analyzed sample efficiency alongside computational cost and continual-learning limitations.

The results were promising but incremental. The lasting lesson was to judge a sampling method by the learning it enables and the overhead it adds. A useful next sample is only part of the problem; choosing a consequential problem and a convincing comparison matters just as much.

The question I carried forward

What information should a model receive next—and can it actually make use of it?

That question connects to my later work on physical-field reconstruction, where a moving sensor’s path determines the observations available to the model.
